\begin{abstract}
Searching a large disaster area with a UAV swarm requires reliable person detection in poor light, continued operation after the loss of an aircraft, and delivery of findings over radio links that are mostly unavailable. Existing coordination and routing methods usually assume a connected network or a central planner. This paper presents a decentralized search-and-rescue system for such conditions that integrates three components: a 4-channel RGB and thermal early-fusion YOLO11 detector, a delay-tolerant mesh with store-carry-forward alert relaying, and a coordination layer that combines energy-weighted centroidal Voronoi tessellation (EW-CVT), dual-threshold hysteresis, sector claiming and lease-based failure detection. Every UAV plans from its own, possibly outdated, view of the swarm. We evaluate the system in a kinematic simulator that uses real multispectral frames, over 1{,}820 paired missions with confidence intervals. Buffering raises the share of victims known to the operator from 3.4\% to 98\% in a 5\,km arena, and failover recovers 11 points of coverage after a UAV is lost. Epidemic relaying delivers alerts 7 to 11\,s sooner than spray-and-wait, PRoPHET and a GeoSaW-style forwarder at 3\,km, but it keeps more copies of each alert. The energy-weighted partition leaves a higher minimum battery than a nearest-bidder auction, but it is not faster. After a loss, the survivors need about 230\,s to detect it, because no UAV can know of the loss before the missing one is overdue at base. All results come from simulation, and we discuss the limits of this evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The first hours after an earthquake or a flood largely determine who is found alive, and the survival rate drops sharply with time \cite{speth2022}. Small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) can cover a wide area much faster than ground teams, and a swarm can cover it faster still. However, several practical problems stand in the way of using a swarm in a real disaster. Visible-light cameras perform poorly in shadow or smoke, where a thermal camera can still show a person. Over areas several kilometres wide the swarm is mostly out of radio contact, so a design that assumes a connected network would deliver almost nothing. A single ground planner would be a single point of failure and would need links that disaster areas rarely offer, whereas UAVs that act on their own may duplicate or abandon work when batteries differ and a UAV is lost unnoticed.

We study perception, communication and control together, because each depends on the others. What a UAV can decide depends on what it has actually heard, and what it hears depends on the radio link and on where the other UAVs are. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An integrated decentralized architecture for search and rescue with intermittently connected swarms, which combines a 4-channel RGB and thermal early-fusion detector (YOLO11) with delay-tolerant alert dissemination and energy-aware coordination.
\item A coordination layer that works on stale local views. It uses an energy-weighted partition (EW-CVT) with dual-threshold hysteresis, claim-and-yield sector assignment, and lease-based failure detection that treats silence as normal and releases work only at base.
\item A delay-tolerant alert mechanism with per-recipient buffers and store-carry-forward relaying, evaluated under a physical radio model.
\item A controlled evaluation over 1{,}820 paired simulated missions, with a component-wise ablation and a comparison with spray-and-wait, PRoPHET, a GeoSaW-style forwarder and a nearest-bidder auction. We report the effects with confidence intervals, including the unfavourable ones.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the methodology, Section~\ref{sec:setup} the setup, and Section~\ref{sec:results} the results. Section~\ref{sec:disc} discusses limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{UAVs for search and rescue.} Small UAVs have been used to support wilderness and disaster search since the field trials of Goodrich et al.\ \cite{goodrich2008}. Surveys cover sensing, task assignment and path planning \cite{lyu2023,cabreira2019}. For several robots, exploration and task allocation have been studied by Burgard et al.\ \cite{burgard2005} and by Gerkey and Matari\'c \cite{gerkey2004}, and auction-based methods such as CBBA \cite{choi2009} decide which robot searches which part of the area. Grøntved et al.\ \cite{grontved2023} adapt CBBA to search and rescue and assume a fully connected network, and Giacomossi et al.\ \cite{giacomossi2026} re-auction the sectors of a failed UAV, but the failure is reported by the UAV itself and the simulation has no radio losses. These methods assume reliable communication or reach agreement by consensus \cite{olfatisaber2007}, whereas our setting resembles flying ad hoc networks with sparse, intermittent links \cite{bekmezci2013,zeng2016}.

\textbf{Multispectral person detection.} Combining visible and thermal images is known to improve pedestrian detection in poor light \cite{hwang2015kaist,liu2016multispectral,zhang2019weakly,jia2021llvip}. Early fusion, in which the two modalities are stacked as input channels, needs little extra computation and is therefore suitable for onboard hardware \cite{speth2022}. We use it on top of the YOLO family \cite{redmon2016yolo,wang2023yolov7,jocher2024yolo11}. Speth et al.\ \cite{speth2022} applied RGB and thermal early fusion to person detection from a drone and released the multispectral dataset that we use, and our detector follows their early-fusion design.

\textbf{Coverage control.} Dividing an area among agents with Voronoi cells and moving each agent to the centroid of its cell (Lloyd iteration) is a standard coverage method \cite{lloyd1982,du1999cvt,cortes2004coverage}. Versions that need only limited-range interaction between agents have also been proposed \cite{cortes2005limited}, as have adaptive and fully decentralized control laws \cite{schwager2009}. In contrast to these works, our agents act on outdated views received over a lossy link and resolve conflicts by claiming sectors. Weighting the distance by a quantity that belongs to each agent gives a weighted Voronoi diagram. A power diagram \cite{aurenhammer1987power} subtracts the weight from the squared distance, and we divide by it instead. Energy-aware versions of Lloyd's method use such weights to give smaller regions to robots with less energy \cite{kwok2007,munir2024}. Our weight represents the battery level in the same way, so we do not claim the weighting itself as new. The weight rule of Munir et al.\ \cite{munir2024} reduces to our case $\alpha=1$ when all UAVs drain at similar rates, so we do not run it separately. We add hysteresis to the updates to avoid chattering when the inputs are noisy.

\textbf{Failure detection.} Deciding that a peer has failed when the channel is unreliable is the problem addressed by unreliable failure detectors \cite{chandra1996}. Accrual detectors \cite{hayashibara2004} rely on heartbeat arrival times, which does not suit a mesh in which silence is normal. Instead, our sortie lease follows the lease idea of Gray and Cheriton \cite{gray1989}. A claim is valid until a stated time, and after that time it can be released.

\textbf{Delay-tolerant networking.} When end-to-end paths rarely exist, nodes can store messages and forward them when they meet other nodes \cite{fall2003dtn}. Epidemic and spray-and-wait routing \cite{vahdat2000epidemic,spyropoulos2005spray} trade the delivery rate against the number of copies and the buffer use, probabilistic variants use the contact history \cite{lindgren2012prophet}, and gossip-style dissemination follows a similar idea \cite{demers1987}. GeoSaW \cite{bujari2018geosaw} forwards alerts of search-and-rescue UAVs to carriers whose planned waypoints pass the destination. We use epidemic forwarding with a buffer cap and do not use planned routes, and Section~\ref{sec:base} compares it with spray-and-wait, PRoPHET and a GeoSaW-style forwarder in the same simulator. UAV network simulators couple flight and network models \cite{baidya2018flynetsim}, and our radio model is a simple log-distance channel \cite{rappaport2002wireless} that is probably conservative where a line of sight is likely \cite{alhourani2014}. Our per-node broker follows the DMLT distributed MQTT brokers \cite{kosaka2023dmlt}, of which only the per-recipient buffering is exercised.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
A team of $N$ UAVs launches from a common base and searches an $L\times L$ arena divided into $K$ sectors with centres $c_s$. UAV $i$ has a position, a battery level $B_i\in[0,B_{\max}]$ and a set of claimed sectors. An unknown set of victims lies in the sectors, and a UAV that scans a sector raises an alert for each victim that it detects. The radio links are lossy and time-varying: the packet error rate between two UAVs depends on their distance (Eq.~\ref{eq:pl}), so the communication graph is mostly disconnected in flight. The ground control station (GCS) is a passive sink at the base.

The objective is to maximize the coverage of the arena and the share of victims whose alert reaches the GCS (the operator recall), while keeping the mission time and the alert latency short. Three constraints apply. A UAV must return to base before its battery falls below a 15\% reserve. There is no central planner, so every decision may use only the information that the UAV has itself received. A UAV may also be lost without notice.

\section{Proposed Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{System Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview of the proposed system. Every UAV runs the same three components. \emph{Perception} turns a multispectral frame of the sector below into victim detections. \emph{Mesh communication} sends alerts and periodic status messages over a lossy radio link, buffers what could not be sent, and carries received alerts onward. \emph{Control} chooses which sectors to search, from the UAV's own battery and its current view of its peers, and builds a route. The ground control station (GCS) is a passive sink: it receives alerts and plans nothing.

The interfaces in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} are narrow: detections go to the mesh as alerts, status messages update the view that control has of its peers, and control hands a route to flight. The aircraft and the radio are simulated, whereas the detection, networking and control logic is the code under test.

\subsection{Multispectral Victim Detection}
The visible (RGB) and thermal long-wave infrared frames are stacked into a four-plane tensor, and the first convolution of the detector takes four input channels. The shipped model is YOLO11s with an input size of 1280\,px, and the frames are $1280{\times}937$. It is trained on the NII-CU Multispectral Aerial Person Detection dataset \cite{speth2022}, with a single person class. We initialise the thermal kernel with the pretrained red-channel kernel, and we train without colour jitter because it has no meaning for a thermal plane. The box centre of a detection is mapped linearly into the bounds of the sector in which the frame was taken, which gives a world coordinate. This is sector-based georeferencing. It is not a camera model.

\subsection{Wireless Channel Model}
The path loss between two UAVs at distance $d$ is modelled by Eq.~\ref{eq:pl}.
\begin{equation}
PL(d)=PL(d_0)+10\,n\log_{10}\tfrac{d}{d_0}+X_\sigma+\Delta_{\text{obs}},
\label{eq:pl}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\text{obs}}=15$\,dB when the link is obstructed (a random 10\% chance beyond 50\,m) and 0 otherwise,
with $PL(d_0)=20\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}}-27.55$, $n=2.5$, $d_0=1$\,m, log-normal shadowing $X_\sigma$ with $\sigma=4$\,dB, and a noise floor of $-90$\,dBm. SNR $=P_{tx}-PL-N_0$ maps to a packet error rate: PER $=0$ above 25\,dB, $1$ below 5\,dB, and $1-(1+e^{-0.5(\text{SNR}-15)})^{-1}$ between. Arenas of 2\,km or more use 33\,dBm at 2.4\,GHz, giving PER $\approx0.5$ at about 520\,m; smaller arenas use 30\,dBm at 5\,GHz (about 220\,m). Against arenas of several kilometres, the swarm is mostly disconnected in flight.

\subsection{Delay-Tolerant Alert Dissemination}
\emph{a) Alert generation and buffering.} When a UAV detects a victim, it makes one direct attempt to reach every other UAV and the GCS. Each failed copy is stored in a per-recipient QoS-1 buffer. The buffer is flushed when the link to that recipient has a PER below 0.2, which in practice happens when the UAV returns to base.

\emph{b) Store-carry-forward relaying.} This is epidemic forwarding \cite{vahdat2000epidemic}: a UAV that \emph{receives} an alert also keeps a copy for each recipient that is not known to hold it, and it hands the copy over when it later meets that recipient with a good link. No UAV needs to be in the right place at the right moment, and an alert survives the loss of its finder. On contact, only the copies that the other side lacks are sent, and a full buffer drops its oldest copy.

\emph{c) Status messages.} Every 5\,s, each UAV broadcasts its position, its committed EW-CVT generator and weight, its state, the time at which its route should bring it home, its sector claims, and the set of sectors that it knows to be scanned. These messages are single-hop and are not buffered, since stale state is not worth delivering late. A receiver merges the scanned sets as grow-only sets, and thus the knowledge about coverage spreads transitively when UAVs meet.

\subsection{Energy-Weighted Territory Partitioning}
Let $B_i$ be the battery level of UAV $i$ and $B_{\max}$ the capacity. The UAV has the weight $w_i=(B_i/B_{\max})^{\alpha}$. A sector with centre $c_s$ is preferred by the UAV with the smallest weighted distance
\begin{equation}
d(s,i)=\frac{\lVert c_s-g_i\rVert^2}{\max(0.01,\,w_i)},
\label{eq:pd}
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ is the committed generator of the UAV. For $\alpha=0$ the partition is a plain Voronoi diagram, and a larger $\alpha$ shrinks the territory of low-battery UAVs. The launch partition is the converged CVT (15 Lloyd iterations).

\subsection{Decentralized Coordination and Failure Detection}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:tick} summarizes one control tick of a UAV, and its steps are described below.
\emph{a) Dual-threshold hysteresis.} Every 5\,s, each UAV computes the centroid $C_i^*$ that its cell should have. It does so over the peers that it \emph{believes} to be active, using their last advertised generators and weights. The UAV commits the new generator and weight only if
\begin{equation}
U_i=\big(\Delta B_i\ge\tau_\epsilon\ \wedge\ \lVert C_i^*-C_i\rVert\ge\tau_\delta\big)\ \vee\ \big(\mathcal N_i(t)\ne\mathcal N_i(t_k)\big),
\label{eq:commit}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_\epsilon=2\%$ of $B_{\max}$, $\tau_\delta=5$\,m, and $\mathcal N_i$ the view of the live set held by the UAV. Without this gate, noisy battery readings would commit a new partition at every tick.

\emph{b) Claim-and-yield sector assignment.} A UAV claims a sector if it is the preferred owner by $d(s,i)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:pd}) using committed values and no other UAV holds a valid claim on the sector. It keeps its existing claims unless a better claim (a smaller $d$, with ties broken by id) reaches it. It may take over the claim of a peer only if it would arrive well before the advertised arrival time of that peer. A UAV that has no claims left takes the nearest unclaimed sector. Routes are greedy nearest-neighbour tours that end at the launch pad.

\emph{c) Lease-based failure detection.} In flight, silence tells a UAV nothing about a peer, because the peer may be out of range or it may have landed. Instead, each peer advertises the time at which its route should bring it home. A peer that is still missing \textit{slack} seconds after this time (60\,s by default) is suspected to be lost, and its claims are released. Since a lost peer looks like one that landed quietly, released work is taken only by UAVs \emph{at base}, which have heard the landed peers. Landed UAVs listen for 10\,s, split the work among themselves by $d(s,i)$, and relaunch if the battery allows. A suspicion is withdrawn when the peer is heard again. A UAV aborts and returns at 15\% battery, and it is lost at 0\%.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Control tick of one UAV, executed every 5\,s from its local view of the swarm.}\label{alg:tick}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE merge any status messages received since the last tick into the view (peers, claims, scanned set)
\STATE \textbf{for} each peer $j$ overdue at base by more than \textit{slack}: mark $j$ suspected; release its claims
\STATE $\mathcal N_i\leftarrow$ self $\cup$ peers believed active (landed peers included only if self is at base)
\STATE read own battery $B_i$; $w_i^*\leftarrow(B_i/B_{\max})^{\alpha}$; $C_i^*\leftarrow$ EW-CVT centroid of my cell over $\mathcal N_i$
\STATE \textbf{if} $U_i$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:commit}) \textbf{then} commit $g_i\leftarrow C_i^*$, $w_i\leftarrow w_i^*$
\STATE keep claims not beaten by a better claim; add free sectors I prefer by $d(s,i)$
\STATE \textbf{if} no claims left: claim nearest unclaimed sector; \textbf{if} at base: also share released work
\STATE \textbf{if} claims changed: rebuild route; advertise route ETA, claims and scanned set
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Simulation Environment}
Six UAVs search a $5\times5$\,km arena that is divided into 25 sectors. Each sector shows one frame of the NII-CU multispectral aerial person detection dataset \cite{speth2022}, whose labels serve as ground truth (36 to 56 victims per world). The UAVs launch from a pad, fly at up to 50\,m/s in transit and 32\,m/s while searching, and drain 0.07 or 0.04\%/s of battery above or below 40\,m/s. The launch batteries are uniform between 20\% and 100\%, and the battery readings have a noise of $\sigma=1.5$ points. In the loss scenario, UAV~2 fails silently at $t=80$\,s.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\emph{Coverage} is the share of sectors that were scanned at least once. \emph{Precision} and \emph{recall} are computed by matching reported victims to the ground truth within 15\,m (one-to-one), and recall is taken over all victims in the arena. \emph{Operator recall} counts only the victims whose alert reached the GCS, and \emph{alert latency} is the delay between detection and arrival at the GCS. \emph{Failover latency} is the time from the injected failure to the first claim of one of its sectors by a survivor. \emph{Duplicate scans} count the sectors that were scanned more than once. \emph{Sector flips} count the claims that a UAV gave up to a better claim, \emph{route changes} count the route rebuilds, and \emph{commits} count the updates approved by the hysteresis.

\subsection{Baseline Methods}
Each baseline replaces one policy in the same simulator, so the missions, radio model and seeds are identical. For forwarding, \emph{epidemic} is our scheme from Section~\ref{sec:method}, in which every UAV copies an alert to each UAV that it meets and that lacks one. \emph{Spray-and-wait} \cite{spyropoulos2005spray} gives each alert $L=3$ copies and halves them at each hand-over. \emph{PRoPHET} \cite{lindgren2012prophet} hands a copy to a peer with a higher delivery predictability for the base ($P_\mathrm{init}=0.75$, $\beta=0.25$, $\gamma=0.98$ per 30\,s). The \emph{GeoSaW-style} forwarder keeps one copy and gives it to a peer whose return time to base is more than 10\,s shorter. It only approximates GeoSaW \cite{bujari2018geosaw}, which uses planned waypoints. \emph{Buffer only} carries each alert home with its finder. For allocation, the \emph{auction} baseline assigns each sector to the nearest UAV (all weights equal to 1) and keeps our lease-based failover. It is a nearest-bidder rule, not a full CBBA \cite{choi2009}. We also test it without failover, and a \emph{static} partition. \emph{Copies per alert} counts the UAVs that hold a copy at the end.

For the detector, we run the shipped model on 150 held-out validation frames (IoU 0.5, confidence 0.25). The \emph{day} condition uses the frames unchanged. The \emph{night} condition emulates a dark scene: the RGB plane is multiplied by 0.12 and Gaussian noise ($\sigma=4$ grey levels) is added, while the thermal plane is untouched, because a thermal camera does not depend on light. The \emph{thermal washout} condition emulates a scene in which people and background have almost the same temperature, for example on a hot afternoon: the thermal plane is compressed towards its mean to 25\% of its contrast and noise ($\sigma=5$) is added, while the RGB plane is untouched. Night therefore removes the visible cue, and washout removes the thermal cue. Both are synthetic degradations of daytime frames and not recorded night or hot-day data. In the \emph{RGB-only} and \emph{thermal-only} conditions, the other plane is set to zero. This is modality-masked inference of one fused network, not a comparison of separately trained models.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol and Reproducibility}
A seed fixes the frame in each sector and every launch battery, so each variant sees the same 20 worlds. We report paired differences with 95\% confidence intervals and claim no effect when an interval contains zero. Missions run until completion or 1{,}500\,s,. All numbers come from scripts in the project repository. A detector evaluation and an ablation harness produce the data, running all 1{,}820 missions in a few minutes once the detections are cached. Every component is a command-line switch of the mission simulator.

\section{Results and Analysis}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Detection Performance}
Table~\ref{tab:perc} shows that the fused model reaches an AP50 of 99.8 in daylight. Fusion is about 11 points better than thermal only, so the visible plane adds information, while removing it at night costs only 5.5 points because thermal carries most of the detection. Washout is the hardest case, because the network relies mainly on thermal: the recall falls to 0.39 while the precision stays at 0.99. The RGB-only case scores zero because the network was never trained without the thermal plane, so it says nothing about the value of RGB, and trained single-modality baselines are still needed (Section~\ref{sec:concl}).

Table~\ref{tab:speth} compares the daytime result with the base paper \cite{speth2022}, which reports YOLOv3 trained on the same dataset (Table~9 there, 485 aligned images with 891 people). Our fused YOLO11s model is ahead of their 4-channel model at AP50 (99.8 against 97.9) and at AP75 (85.8 against 76.9), and, like theirs, it is clearly ahead of the RGB-only and ensemble variants. The comparison is indicative only. We evaluate 150 of the 485 frames (275 people) and not all of them. The architecture (YOLO11s at 1280\,px against YOLOv3 at 960$\times$704) and the training schedule differ, so part of the gap comes from the newer detector and not from the fusion. We did not compute AP50:95, and the checkpoint was selected on the same split that we evaluate. The latencies are not comparable (CPU here, a drone-class GPU in \cite{speth2022}).



\subsection{Reference Mission Performance}
Without a failure, the decentralized swarm covers 100\% of the arena in \pmsd{353}{31}\,s (mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over 20 seeds). Precision, recall and F1 are all \pmsd{0.980}{0.015}, where the whole-arena recall counts the victims in unsearched sectors. The mean localization error is \pmsd{1.45}{0.15}\,m, the first victim is detected after \pmsd{8.6}{7.2}\,s, and the operator learns of 100\% of the reports.

\emph{Failover} matters when a UAV is lost. The coverage after the loss is 98.4\% with failover and 87.4\% without it (Fig.~\ref{fig:covnet}, left), since without failover the sectors of the lost UAV are never searched.

\emph{Hysteresis} reduces churn but not flight time. In the full decentralized system it has almost no measurable effect, because the claims are already sticky (0.2 versus 0.4 sector flips). It matters when partitions are recomputed from fresh battery readings at every tick, which we tested with a global planner that recomputes all partitions at each tick. There, hysteresis cuts the partition commits about tenfold (296 to 31 per mission at $\sigma=2\%$) and the sector flips from 13.5 to 2.5, while the mission time stays within noise. It protects against noisy inputs but does not speed up the mission.

\emph{EW weighting} is a trade-off. When $\alpha$ is swept from 0 to 3, the minimum final battery rises from 15.6\% to 20.3\% and the low-battery aborts fall from 0.95 to 0.30 per mission, but the mission becomes longer (377 to 488\,s) because the healthy UAVs carry more of the work. At $\alpha=1$ the benefit is modest ($+0.8\pm0.7$ points of minimum battery).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}
Our evidence clearly supports two conclusions. A sparsely connected swarm needs buffering, and losing a UAV costs coverage unless the others take over its work, with a delay set by how long the survivors need to learn of the loss. Other results are weaker than the design suggests. Hysteresis has an effect only where the partitions are recomputed from noisy readings at every tick, and energy weighting buys reserve at the price of speed.

Several limitations apply. First, the simulator is kinematic, with constant speeds, linear battery drain, no wind and no obstacles beyond a probabilistic radio penalty. Second, each sector shows one static dataset frame, so the mission-level precision and recall mostly reflect the detector on these frames and the sectors that were visited, and the localization error measures a mapping from box to sector. Third, the perception ablation masks one plane of a single fused network, and the training and validation frames come from the same site, so the absolute numbers are probably optimistic. Fourth, the network results depend on the radio model. Fifth, the decentralized design is simplified: the UAVs launch together and share the initial partition, status messages are single-hop, alerts are addressed to every UAV whether alive or not, and forwarding reads the holdings of the receiver instead of simulating the exchange on contact. The lease detector is one of several possible designs and has not been compared with the others. Our auction baseline is not a full CBBA, and our GeoSaW-style forwarder uses no planned waypoints. Finally, the 15\% reserve and the noise levels are scenario assumptions.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:concl}
This paper presented a decentralized search-and-rescue system for swarms that are mostly out of radio contact. It integrates early-fusion RGB and thermal perception, a delay-tolerant mesh with store-carry-forward relaying, and an energy-aware coordination layer without a central planner. Buffering was the largest effect that we measured. Failover recovers coverage after a UAV is lost, and epidemic relaying delivers alerts faster than the baselines that we tried, at the price of more copies. The energy-weighted partition leaves more battery reserve but is not faster than a nearest-bidder auction, and the price of decentralization is a failover latency of minutes. We have reported the unfavourable results and the limits of a simulation-only study.

Future work has three directions. First, separate RGB-only and thermal-only detectors should be trained for a proper modality ablation, and a full CBBA and complete prior-work stacks should be compared across a sweep of network connectivity, which would test the integration claim directly. Second, status messages could carry third-party state, so that failure detection does not wait for an overdue return. Third, position-dependent imagery, a camera model and richer wind, payload and energy models would bring the simulation closer to real flight.
